Educational challenge

Better output is not the same as better learning

Generative AI can help students produce text, solve problems and receive feedback at unprecedented speed. Yet the same systems can also make them appear more capable while removing the struggle through which capability is formed.

Hybrid Intelligence in Education offers a different approach. Rather than treating AI as either a threat to learning or a shortcut to efficiency, it asks how human intelligence and artificial intelligence can be deliberately combined so that learning, judgment and human agency are strengthened.

The goal is not simply to teach students how to use AI tools. It is to build up learners so they can frame problems, explore alternatives, evaluate machine-generated outputs, make responsible decisions and redesign the systems and workflows in which AI is used.

Education is not only where society responds to technological change. It is one of the institutions through which society can actively shape it.

Protecting the expertise pipeline

AI can improve short-term performance while weakening the pathways through which professional expertise is developed.

When entry-level tasks are increasingly delegated to AI, learners and early-career professionals may lose opportunities to build the contextual and tacit knowledge required for later judgment. Organizations can become more productive in the present while becoming less capable of reproducing the human expertise they will need in the future.

Education therefore carries a wider responsibility than merely preparing individuals to use new tools. It must help society continuously reproduce the expertise required to evaluate, govern and improve AI-supported work.

The central risk

A society may become increasingly capable of producing outputs while becoming less capable of developing the people who can understand, challenge and take responsibility for them.

From AI literacy to hybrid competence

Learning to work at the prediction-judgment frontier

AI literacy remains important: learners must understand how AI works, critically evaluate its outputs and consider its ethical and societal implications. But literacy alone does not determine how responsibility should be distributed inside an AI-supported task.

AI is especially effective when work can be performed through abundant, explicit and formalized information. Human judgment becomes essential when a task depends on context, tacit knowledge, values, interpretation or accountable ownership.

The boundary is dynamic. As contextual knowledge becomes externalized and machine-readable, parts of yesterday’s judgment become today’s prediction. Education therefore cannot rely on a fixed catalogue of uniquely human skills. It must cultivate the capacity to identify where AI prediction is useful, where human judgment is required and where responsibility must remain human.

Prediction points

Moments where AI can generate, classify, simulate, search or accelerate a clearly bounded part of the work.

Judgment points

Moments where learners must frame, interpret, validate, choose, escalate or take responsibility for consequences.

How this relates to existing frameworks

The Five Big Ideas presented by AI4K12, the DigComp lineage and UNESCO’s competency frameworks have established that learners should understand how AI systems work, evaluate their outputs and weigh their societal implications.

The joint OECD / European Union AILit framework goes further. Its Manage AI domain asks learners to decide where AI should automate or augment and to set checkpoints between human and AI roles; its Shape AI domain casts them as improvers of AI systems rather than consumers of them.

Hybrid Intelligence education departs from the existing frameworks in two respects. Where AILit anchors the human contribution in a catalogue of capacities (emotion, ethical reasoning, critical thinking, originality), we anchor it in the knowledge structure of the task, which does not age each time a capability gets better. And where AILit prescribes iteration as a general principle, we specify a governed procedure, FERC, that makes framing, comparison and accountable commitment assessable rather than dependent on individual disposition.

The calibration dilemma

What should education preserve, and what should it redesign?

Not every traditional learning activity should disappear because AI can perform parts of it. Learners still need independent mastery wherever professional practice requires unaided reasoning, embodied skill or real-time judgment.

At the same time, education should not prepare students only for workflows that are already being transformed. Institutions must continuously assess which capabilities should remain independently mastered and which should be developed through structured human–AI collaboration.

Preserve independent mastery

Retain deep knowledge and unaided competence where future practice still depends on direct human reasoning, physical skill or immediate judgment.

Redesign for hybrid practice

Rework learning activities where AI will be part of professional practice, so students learn to frame, validate, refine and take responsibility.

The graduate this produces

The faglig kaospilot

A domain expert trained not just to survive labour-market turbulence, but to steer it. By grounding the living-laboratory mission in real organizational experimentation, this programme yields graduates who carry the capacity to move workplaces from basic AI automation toward durable human–AI collaboration.

Instead of education trailing behind market shifts, these graduates are the direct mechanism by which institutions actively lead the transition.

Institutional transformation

The HI Educational Transformation Playbook

The playbook connects institutional purpose, shared mindsets, educational practice and formal assessment. It helps institutions move from isolated experiments towards a coherent capacity for Hybrid Intelligence.

Governed human–AI collaboration

FERC keeps the learner in the author’s seat

Many approaches to AI in education focus on writing better prompts. FERC instead structures the full interaction through four phases: Frame, Explore, Refine and Commit. The process begins with human intent, creates space for comparison and refinement, and ends with accountable human commitment.

Shift the focus from the perfect prompt to the perfect collaboration.

FERC is developed as a separate HI framework with its own research, practical guidance and examples.

From designed learning to learner designing

Seamful co-creation

Educational AI often arrives as finished furniture: an approved chatbot, a fixed prompt template or a predefined set of rules. This is an appropriate starting point, but it should not be the endpoint.

Learners who are expected to enter AI-rich organizations must also practise designing the conditions under which AI is used. They should be able to define task boundaries, configure instructions and criteria, identify uncertainty, create escalation points and decide where human review is structurally required.

This is the idea of seamful co-creation. Instead of hiding every limitation and handover, the learning design makes important seams visible and actionable. Friction is not added everywhere; it is placed where thinking, evaluation and responsibility need to occur.

What it looks like in practice

The classroom as a laboratory for Hybrid Intelligence

The playbook does not prescribe one technology. It provides activity formats in which AI expands the field of possibilities while learners exercise contextual reasoning, epistemic vigilance and accountable judgment.

Iterative bot or prompt co-design

Students test an AI tutor, debate partner, case coach or workflow simulator against explicit quality criteria. They identify weaknesses, revise its instructions and test it again.

Virtual stakeholder exploration

AI generates stakeholders, personas or scenario variations that widen the analytical field. Students interrogate assumptions, assess realism, surface trade-offs and decide which perspectives should shape the final proposal.

Flawed-bot detection and repair

An AI configuration contains a deliberate misconception, weak assumption or bias. Students diagnose the flaw, articulate the relevant domain knowledge and redesign the prompt or workflow.

HI business process re-engineering

Students select a professional workflow, map the prediction-judgment boundary, generate alternative human–AI configurations and commit to a defensible redesign. The exercise demonstrates why domain expertise remains essential: without contextual judgment, AI proposals tend to remain plausible but generic.

Objectives and assessment

Assess judgment, not only task completion

Generative AI weakens the link between a polished submitted artifact and the learning it was intended to demonstrate. A strong final output may conceal weak framing, uncritical delegation or little durable understanding.

HI learning objectives are therefore written as observable judgment behaviour: validate, detect, justify, stress-test, integrate and assume accountability. Foundational knowledge is not reduced. It becomes the basis for informed evaluation of AI-supported work.

DomainTraditional learning objectiveHI learning objective
Trade and logisticsProcess customer orders correctly in an ERP system and ensure accurate invoicing.Validate and critically assess AI-generated order proposals; detect anomalies and justify override decisions in light of pricing patterns, customer history and operational risk.
Business and marketingProduce a complete market analysis report.Evaluate, refine and justify an AI-supported market analysis, identifying its assumptions, risks and strategic trade-offs.
EngineeringCalculate structural load requirements using standard formulas.Assess and stress-test AI-assisted load calculations, identify boundary conditions, and justify final design decisions under uncertainty.
HealthcareDevelop a treatment plan based on patient symptoms and clinical guidelines.Critically evaluate AI-generated treatment suggestions, integrate patient-specific factors, and assume accountable clinical decision-making.

Across domains, the shift is from completing predictable tasks towards validating, contextualizing and responsibly finalizing AI-supported work.

Process-based assessment

Assessment can include the learner’s original frame, the alternatives explored, comparative evaluation, revisions, the documented role of AI and the rationale behind the final commitment.

Employability and formation point in the same direction

Problem framing, contextual judgment and accountable commitment are not only workplace competences. They also support autonomy, co-determination and responsible participation in society. HI Education therefore connects professional relevance with the broader formative purpose of education.

Re-professionalizing teaching

Teacher judgment becomes more important, not less

When submitted work no longer certifies the learning it was designed to evidence, output-based assessment loses its warrant. The answer is not better detection. It is to shift evaluative weight onto what can still be observed: whether a learner framed the problem before generating, demanded and compared genuine alternatives, revised their criteria, and committed with a documented rationale.

Evaluating how a learner frames a problem, compares alternatives and justifies a decision cannot be reduced to a judgment-free metric. It requires professional teacher judgment.

That cuts against three decades in which educational governance has drifted from professional responsibility towards measurable output. Hybrid education asks institutions to reverse part of that drift: to reinvest in, and re-trust, the judgment of educators. The cost is real: professional development, redesigned assessment, and protected time.

Students learn what their institutions model.

Institutional maturity

From isolated use to institutional identity

Institutions begin from different positions. The maturity model provides a shared developmental horizon: from AI being peripheral, through local experimentation and operationalized practices, to Hybrid Intelligence becoming part of institutional strategy and identity.

  1. 4

    Transformative

    Strategic DNA

    Human–AI collaboration is embedded in the institution’s identity. Teaching is proactively redesigned to strengthen graduates’ future readiness and to help shape a more human-centred society. HI is not an add-on; it is strategic orientation.

    “Human–AI partnership is our identity.”

  2. 3

    Operationalized

    Stable hybrid culture

    AI use is guided by shared institutional norms and detailed principles that ensure quality and responsibility. Prediction and judgment are systematically embedded in activities.

    “AI collaboration is professional excellence.”

  3. 2

    Emerging

    Individual experimentation

    AI use becomes iterative. Individuals refine prompts and experiment continuously, but practice remains personal rather than institutionally structured.

    “AI is legitimate, but it depends on skill.”

  4. 1

    Initiating

    One-shot

    AI is used sporadically in isolated interactions. A single prompt is tried, and the response is either accepted directly or abandoned.

    “Is this cheating?”

  5. 0

    Inactive

    AI is not used in teaching or preparation and is rarely discussed with students or colleagues. It is seen as irrelevant or peripheral.

    “AI is irrelevant, or a threat.”

The maturity journey asks whether hybrid competence is individually improvised or collectively cultivated and governed.

Equity and access

Hybrid competence must not become a new privilege

Judgment-centred education makes a demand that AI-literacy modules do not. It presupposes learners who can reach configurable AI systems, teachers with the time and training to design judgment points into their own subjects, and institutions able to govern how those systems are used. Where those conditions are missing, the framework does not simply fail to arrive. It can widen the gap it was meant to close.

Access to configurable systems

Prompt-level access to a consumer chatbot is not the same as the ability to configure, constrain and inspect a system, and that tooling is currently distributed by budget rather than by educational need.

Teacher time and capacity

Process-based assessment costs professional time. Institutions that adopt HI objectives without funding that time will produce the paperwork of hybrid education and none of the learning.

The risk is a two-tier settlement: well-resourced institutions teaching students to design and govern AI systems, while others teach students to consume their outputs. We therefore treat access as part of the framework rather than as an implementation detail, and we would rather see this playbook adopted partially and fairly than fully and narrowly.
